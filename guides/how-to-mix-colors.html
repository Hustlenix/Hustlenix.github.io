Practical mixing

How to mix colors deliberately instead of guessing

Published September 2026 · Five-step method

Random mixing can be fun, but it is a poor way to learn control. A better method is to compare your current color with the target, identify the biggest difference, and make one small correction at a time. This works with physical paint, digital painting, and the mixing system inside Infinite Colour Craft.

Step 1: match value before chasing hue

Ask whether your color is too light or too dark. If the value is badly wrong, even a perfect hue may still look incorrect in context. Squint at the reference, temporarily view it in grayscale, or compare it against a neutral background.

With physical paint, white usually raises value and black lowers it, but both can also reduce saturation. In digital tools, changing value directly may preserve saturation more cleanly. The principle is the same: fix the largest light-dark error first.

Step 2: move the hue in a direction, not toward a name

Instead of thinking “make this brown,” ask “does this need to move more toward orange, yellow, red, green, blue, or violet?” Directional thinking is easier to correct because you can see whether the last change moved you closer or farther away.

If a red is too purple, move it slightly toward orange. If a green is too yellow, move it slightly toward blue. Small directional adjustments are more reliable than adding a large amount of a guessed color.

Step 3: control saturation

If the hue is roughly right but the color still feels artificial, saturation is often the issue. Real scenes contain many muted colors. A wall described as “blue” may actually be a grey-blue. Skin, foliage, clouds, soil, and fabric frequently contain less saturation than beginners expect.

You can reduce saturation by moving toward a neutral, mixing a complement carefully, or choosing a greyer version of the same hue. Avoid immediately adding black; black changes value as well as chroma and can make a mixture muddy faster than intended.

Good mixing rule: change one variable at a time. If you change hue, value, and saturation together, you will not know which change helped.

Step 4: check temperature

Two colors can have similar hue names while feeling different because one leans warmer or cooler. A green can lean toward yellow and feel warm, or toward blue and feel cool. A red can lean toward orange or toward violet.

Temperature is especially useful when painting light. Warm light often creates relatively cooler shadow relationships, while cool light can make lit areas feel cooler and surrounding accents warmer. These are relationships, not fixed laws, so observe the specific scene.

Step 5: make the final adjustment smaller than you think

Many bad mixtures happen because the correction is too large. Once you are close, use tiny changes. In physical paint that might mean touching the tip of a brush into the adjusting color. Digitally it might mean shifting a slider only a few points.

The closer you are to the target, the more cautious each correction should become.

Mix from a limited palette

A limited palette forces you to understand relationships because you cannot solve every problem by choosing a fresh swatch. Try working with one warm primary, one cool primary, a yellow, white, and a dark neutral. You may not reproduce every possible pigment perfectly, but you will learn how colors push and pull each other.

Infinite Colour Craft starts with a small set for the same reason: scarcity makes each new combination informative.

Keep a mixing log

If you are practicing physically, place a small sample of each mixture on paper and write what you added. If you are working digitally, save a palette strip. The goal is not to memorize hundreds of recipes; it is to notice patterns such as “this blue dominates quickly” or “this yellow raises warmth without changing value much.”

Do not judge a color alone

A mixture can look wrong on white and correct inside the painting. Human vision compares neighboring colors. A medium grey can look warm beside blue and cool beside orange. Test your mixture in the actual context where it will be used.

Ten-minute drill

  1. Pick a target color from a photo.
  2. Make an initial guess.
  3. Compare value and correct it.
  4. Compare hue direction and correct it.
  5. Compare saturation and correct it.
  6. Place the result next to surrounding colors from the photo.
  7. Make only one final small temperature adjustment.

Repeat with a different target. The important part is naming the reason for every adjustment. After enough repetitions, color mixing becomes less like gambling and more like debugging.
