Skill practice

Four digital painting exercises that build real color intuition

Published September 2026 · 10–20 minutes each

Improvement comes faster when an exercise isolates one decision. If you try to practice anatomy, perspective, rendering, color, composition, and brushwork in one finished illustration, it becomes difficult to tell what actually improved. These four drills deliberately remove choices so you can concentrate on color and value.

Exercise 1: three-value study

Goal: make an image readable using only a dark, a middle, and a light.

  1. Choose a photo with a clear subject.
  2. Create exactly three neutral values.
  3. Reduce every shape in the reference to one of those values.
  4. Ignore texture and detail.
  5. At the end, shrink the study until it is thumbnail-sized. The subject should still read.

This drill teaches grouping. Beginners often create too many tiny value changes, which makes the image noisy. Three groups force you to decide which forms belong together.

Once the study works, repeat it with three colors that preserve the same light-dark structure. Notice how much of the image remains readable even though the hues changed.

Exercise 2: one hue, five saturations

Goal: learn to see saturation independently from value.

  1. Pick one hue, such as blue.
  2. Create five swatches from vivid blue to nearly neutral blue-grey.
  3. Try to keep their value similar.
  4. Paint a simple object using only those five swatches.

The difficulty is keeping the swatches equally light or dark while reducing intensity. This makes saturation a conscious variable rather than something that changes accidentally whenever you move a color picker.

For a second pass, reverse the hierarchy: use the most saturated swatch only in the smallest important area. The painting should immediately gain a focal point.

Exercise 3: warm light, cool shadow — then reverse it

Goal: understand temperature as a relationship.

Paint a sphere, cube, or simple object. In the first version, make the lit side relatively warm and the shadow side relatively cool. In the second version, reverse the relationship. Keep the values approximately the same between both studies.

The point is not to memorize “light is warm and shadow is cool.” That is not always true. The point is to feel how temperature contrast changes atmosphere without relying on value contrast alone.

Tip: compare temperature inside one hue family first. A yellow-green can be warmer than a blue-green even though both are still called green.

Exercise 4: hard, soft, and lost edges

Goal: stop outlining every form equally.

Paint a simple object against a background and deliberately use three edge types:

  • Hard edge: a crisp transition that attracts attention.
  • Soft edge: a gradual transition that suggests turning form, atmosphere, or shallow depth.
  • Lost edge: foreground and background become so similar that the boundary nearly disappears.

Color is affected by edges because contrast determines what the eye notices. A highly saturated patch with a hard edge demands attention. The same patch softened into nearby colors becomes calmer.

Use a timer

Short time limits are useful because they prevent polishing from taking over. Set ten or fifteen minutes and stop when the timer ends. You are training decisions, not producing portfolio work.

After each study, write one sentence: “The biggest mistake was…” and one sentence: “Next time I will…” That small review turns repetition into deliberate practice.

What brushes should you use?

For color practice, simpler is usually better. Use one opaque brush for the first three exercises so texture does not distract you. For the edge exercise, use one hard brush and one soft or smudge tool.

Infinite Colour Craft includes multiple tools because experimentation is part of the game, but when you are training a specific skill, limiting your tools makes feedback clearer.

How to use Infinite Colour Craft for these drills

You can use the game's Paint Studio after discovering a small palette. Instead of unlocking as many colors as possible, deliberately restrict yourself:

  • For the value study, choose one dark, one middle, and one light color.
  • For saturation practice, build a family of related colors and order them from vivid to muted.
  • For temperature studies, choose neighboring warm and cool variants.
  • For edges, combine the brush, smudge, and eraser tools.

The game loop becomes more useful when discovery feeds a specific painting exercise rather than ending at the unlock screen.

Measure progress by decisions, not prettiness

A study can look rough and still be successful. If you can identify values faster, mute a color without destroying its value, or control where the sharpest edge appears, the exercise worked. Those skills transfer to larger drawings later.
